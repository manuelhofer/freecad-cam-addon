4-axis machining

A part is milled out of a round bar while the bar turns – on a mill with a rotary axis (A or B) just as on a lathe with a C axis and driven tools. The wizard gets you there in two steps: placing the part in the bar and then “What do you want to do?” – All-round roughing and All-round finishing, all around or only over the faces you click.

How it works

  1. Open the document with the part and, in the 3D view, click the flat face that should sit at the front of the bar – usually the end face.
  2. In the “CAM-Addon” toolbar, click 4-axis machining. The part moves into a see-through bar and turns once about the axis.
  3. Enter the bar diameter, say 80. The line at the bottom says in green how much material is left all round at least – or in red which diameter the part would need.
  4. Next – step 2: What do you want to do? “All-round roughing” is ticked; below are cutter, use case and values from the tool manager and, in grey, how many layers it takes – say “→ 5 layers (Ø 80.0 mm → Ø 60.6 mm)”. “All-round finishing” is ticked when a cutter in the tool manager has a “Finishing” application. The fields of a section are shown while it is ticked – otherwise only its title, the explanation and, in grey, why it works or not.
  5. Create – the CAM job with the part in the bar, the tool controllers and the operations “All-round roughing T1” and “All-round finishing T2” appear; their paths run round the part. The first Ctrl+Z undoes controllers and operations, the second job and bar. Cancel leaves nothing behind.

Back returns to the stock. Without a tick, “Create” only creates job and bar.

Clicking another face while the window is open puts the part into the bar with that face at the front.

How the part sits in the bar

  • Front is the free end of the bar, away from the chuck. The chosen face sits there, with the bar sticking out by the face allowance. The zero along the axis lies in this face, the part behind it – like Z0 on a lathe.
  • Centre of the round face – if the outer edge of the face is a circle, its centre sits on the bar axis (the centroid for a face with corners). That way a shaft stays round even when a cam sticks out.
  • Whole part as central as possible – the part is placed so that it fits the thinnest bar.
  • Next to both you see the diameter the bar needs for it. The round face is preselected if the part fits the bar that way, otherwise the whole part.
  • Rotation turns the part in the bar about the axis and so sets where 0° of the rotary axis is.

The bar

Behind the part come the parting width (room for parting off or sawing) and the clamping length (how far the bar sits in the chuck). Together they give the length of the bar, shown in grey next to the fields.

Everything can be set, and empty fields use their grey suggestion. What you enter becomes next time's suggestion. The bar diameter depends on the part. Its suggestion is the next 5 mm step (1/8" in inch) that leaves at least 1 mm all round.

Machine and rotary axis

Right below the part the wizard asks: which machine? The axis the bar turns about decides how it lies in the job – and the machine sets it.

  • An open machine (“Edit machine”) is listed and preselected. Below it in grey, what it can do: “Linear axes X1, Y1, Z1 · rotary axis for the bar: C · 12 tool stations”. The rotary axis below is the machine's – if it has only one, it cannot be changed. The bar then lies in the job the way it is clamped in that machine: on a lathe along the spindle, the front away from the chuck. “Check on machine” shows it exactly like that and uses the same machine for checking (if it is saved).
  • The machine used last – the one you last checked on – is offered as “Open “…” (last used)”, even when it is not open. Choosing it opens it; the 3D view stays with the part. So are your machines from the list Machines … whose rotary axis turns the bar – lathes, 4- and 5-axis mills: “Open “…””.
  • You come here from the assistant Machining (milling) when you choose a lathe in its step 1 (“Continue in the 4-axis assistant →”): with the part, the clicked face and this machine, preselected.
  • A machine without a rotary axis for the bar (a 3-axis mill) is shown greyed out: “… – no rotary axis for the bar”.
  • Without a machine you assign the letter to the bar yourself: A – the bar lies along X, the tool comes from above (a mill with rotary axis A); B – along Y, the tool from above; C – along Z, the tool from X, like a lathe with a C axis.

The bar axis runs through the job's origin. That way FreeCAD lays later paths correctly around the part by itself.

Step 2: faces

Without a selection the part is milled all around – every outer face. To mill single faces only: in step 2 click a face of the part in 3D; clicking it again removes it (a double-click in the list does too).

  • The list names every selected face with its kind (“Plane”, “Cylinder Ø 20.00 mm, centred” …) and how far a tool pointing from outside at the axis gets to it: green reachable, yellow partly (“50 % reachable”), red not – under an overhang or across the axis. In the 3D view the selected faces show these colours; after the window the part looks as before.
  • All outer faces selects every face that faces outwards – that is all around. Clear selection does the same.
  • With selected faces “All-round roughing” and “All-round finishing” only mill where the cutter touches one of them – so it also reaches their edges. The calculation still uses the whole part: it stays intact. The cutter moves in rows back and forth: each row across the face, at its end one step along the axis, and the rotary axis turns back. It only lifts between separate faces. It enters by rapid down to just above what is left there, then along a ramp with the cutter's plunge angle from the tool manager (5° if none is given) – when finishing straight down at the plunge feed. When roughing and for “lines along the axis” you can choose climb milling only (lift, start again) – every row on its own, climb milling, the cutter lifts in between.
  • Both operations remember the faces (property “Flaechen”); when changing they are back in the list.

Step 2: all-round roughing

The bar is removed all round down to the finishing allowance – in layers, with a cutter pointing at the axis from outside while the rotary axis turns.

  • Material – which one the cutting data of both operations are for: the stock's if it has one, otherwise the one used last.
  • Cutter – from the tool manager: end, torus, ball, slot or face mill with cutting data for the material. Preselected is the one used last, otherwise the first end mill. If there is none, a red line says so; Tool manager … opens it – once you save there, it is available. Ball and torus mills are calculated like an end mill of the same diameter – the part stays safe; between the turns of the spiral ridges remain at their roundings, and a grey sentence says how high (ball Ø 10 at 4 mm per revolution: 0.42 mm above the allowance). Less feed per revolution makes them flatter. If a machine is chosen at the top and the cutter would not sit radially to the bar on its station, a yellow sentence below says so – with the holder that is missing (such as “VDI30 driven radial”); how a tool stands is up to its holder in the tool manager. If the machine has a turret, each cutter starts with the station it sits in or gets in this job (“P3 · T1 End mill …”): its station in the job, otherwise the one of its number if free, otherwise the first free one. In the program it is called like its station (see Tool loading).
  • Use case – “Roughing” preselected. It gives spindle speed and feed (grey below) and the suggestions for the fields.
  • Step-down per layer – how deep each layer cuts radially (the use case's ap). Feed per revolution – how far the spiral advances along the bar per revolution (the use case's ae, at most the diameter). Finishing allowance – what stays all round (0.3 mm suggested).

Distances – apply to roughing and finishing:

  • Overrun – how far the centre of the cutter runs past the back of the part so that it leaves it completely and the rear edge gets finished. Empty, each cutter uses its radius + 0.5 mm (grey “radius + 0.5”, 6.5 mm for a 12 mm cutter); a number applies to both.
  • Chuck distance – how far the edge of the cutter stays in front of the clamping face (5 mm suggested). If the cutter has a holder in the tool manager that reaches further sideways beyond the tool axis than the cutter – such as the head of “VDI30 driven radial”, 27.5 mm –, its edge stays that far in front of the chuck. Safety distance – how far above and in front of the bar it moves in rapid (2 mm suggested).

How far the bar sticks out of the chuck follows from this – it is stated in grey: “The bar has to stick out 78.5 mm from the chuck: facing allowance 1.0 + part 60.0 + overrun 6.5 + cutter radius 6.0 + chuck distance 5.0.” The wizard creates the bar that long (plus the clamping length in the chuck); clamp it that way on the machine. The cutter that needs more room behind the part counts; if its holder reaches further than the cutter, it says “holder beyond the tool axis 27.5” instead of the cutter radius. Without an operation the part-off width is enough.

The path: each layer is a spiral from the front – the cutter fully in front of the bar – to the overrun behind the part; there the tip stays at the depth of the last piece of contour. Climb milling: the rotary axis turns so that the material lies to the right of the motion, seen from the spindle – one way with M3, the other with M4 (“Reverse” on the tool controller); which direction of C or A that is, the add-on works out from the machine’s sense of rotation. The same for the walls of a slot on the circumference, cross holes and keyways. The edge of the cutter – or of its holder, if that reaches further – always stays the chuck distance in front of the clamping face. The operation remembers how far the holder reaches (property “Halter Zum Futter”). Where the part is higher than the layer, the tip follows the part plus allowance; it never comes closer to the axis than the cutter radius. In front of every wall across the axis – a shoulder, the flank of a groove – the spiral pauses for one revolution (ring pass): that way the cutter reaches the wall all round; otherwise it would only touch it on part of the circumference. Between layers it goes out radially and back to the front in rapid. It is calculated against the whole part, meshed to 0.02 mm: the error goes into the allowance, never into the part.

In the program – please read before the first run:

  • X in diameter or in radius – the way your machine counts (the box “counts in diameter (Ø)” on the axis, “X as” in “New machine”); the check window shows it the same way. FreeCAD's own post processors always write X as a radius, though: if your machine counts in diameter, switch the program header to radius (Siemens DIAMOF) until the add-on's post processor writes X in diameter – otherwise the tool would go to half the distance from the axis. With A and B, Z is the distance from the axis.
  • The feed is given after G93 (1 ÷ time) – so the cutter runs at the use case's feed on the workpiece, however far it is from the axis. G94 follows. The post processor must write F in every block and output metric.
  • FreeCAD's post processors (checked with 1.1.3 and 26.3): LinuxCNC, Mach3/Mach4, Masso G3, Generic (26.3), KineticNC/Beamicon2, Estlcam, Dynapath 4060, Fanuc and UCCNC write G93, C and F as they must. (Fanuc and UCCNC drop an F that equals the previous one – so two equal ones never follow each other in the program; the difference is in the 6th decimal place.) Centroid, Smoothie, Fablin and Philips drop the rotary axis, Fangling the F – they are no use for this.
  • On a lathe these post processors write M3 S… – there that is the main spindle. The program header switches on the driven tool and C-axis mode (Siemens e.g. SETMS(…) with the number of the tool drive, otherwise the machine builder's M codes). On Fanuc lathes with G code system A, G94 is a facing cycle and feed per minute is G98 – there an adapted post processor is needed.
  • The rotary axis keeps counting (−3600° and more): it must run without modulo, otherwise it would not turn round several times.
  • At the start the tool moves out radially to the safety distance, then to the front of the bar and across to the centre (Y0 – not if the machine has no Y axis).

The values sit on the operation in the group “4-Achs” and can be changed there; the path is then recalculated.

Step 2: all-round finishing

After roughing, one spiral runs on the part: the tip follows the surface plus allowance – calculated with the cutter's real shape, meshed to 0.005 mm and exactly at the points of the spiral. In front of every wall across the axis it pauses for one revolution, as in roughing; in the corner only the fillet that the cutter's shape leaves remains (for a ball mill its radius).

  • Cutter – any from the tool manager whose shape the add-on knows: ball, torus, flat, tapered ball, lollipop, chamfer, slitting, face and dovetail mills; the radius mill is calculated with the chord of its profile (it stays higher). Preselected is the one used last, otherwise one with a “Finishing” application – ball before torus.
  • Application – “Finishing” preselected; it gives speed and feed.
  • Stepover – how far the spiral advances per revolution. Empty, the grey ae of the application from the tool table applies. Below it is the scallop height left between two passes (ball Ø 6 at 0.3 mm: 0.004 mm).
  • Allowance – what stays; 0 finishes the part.
  • Pattern – how the path runs: spiral all round, or lines along the axis at a fixed angle, alternating in direction, spaced by the stepover on the largest radius – for faces that do not go all round (flat, groove, cam): there the spiral would only run rows across the face, the lines follow it lengthwise. Suggested is what fits the selected faces; the reason is shown in grey below (“Suggestion: lines along the axis – the selected faces do not go all round.”). If you want otherwise, choose it.
  • climb milling only (lift, start again) – only with lines along the axis: off, the cutter runs the lines back and forth, every other one conventional. On, it runs every line on its own in the same direction (with M3 from the chuck towards the front, with M4 the other way), lifts at its end and enters again at the start of the next – the surface becomes more even, it takes longer. The spiral always runs climb milling.

In grey below: how many revolutions the spiral has and roughly how long it takes – say “→ 365 revolutions, about 1 h 35 min”, for lines along the axis “→ 126 lines along the axis, about 12 min”. Without “All-round roughing” before it (ticked or already in the job) finishing does not work: it would take the whole bar in one pass.

Where the roughing cutter could not quite reach – a narrow groove, the wedges its spiral leaves in an inside corner – finishing first removes material in stages: each at most the radius of its cutter deep (at least the roughing allowance + 0.5 mm), only over the revolutions where something is left there; in between it lifts off at rapid. How many there are shows at the operation (“Vorstufen”) and in the report view. The time in the wizard leaves them out – the operation shows the real revolutions once created.

Step 2: indexed facing

For a flat face along the bar – a flat, a wrench flat, the floor of a slot: the rotary axis turns the face towards the cutter, and a cutter with a flat tip mills it like face milling – rows along the axis, stepping across by the row spacing (with Y for C), in layers from the top down to the face. This makes it flat right into the corners; with the cutter on the ray from the axis (spiral, lines along the axis) its tip would sit at an angle to the face and something would remain at the edge. Needs an axis across the bar on the machine (Y for C).

  • It is suggested ticked as soon as a selected face lies flat along the bar – the reason is shown in grey below. Without such a face or without a cross axis the tick is locked, and the sentence says why.
  • Cutter – flat, torus or face mill (flat tip) from the tool manager; Application – “Facing” preselected.
  • Stepdown per layer (ap), row spacing (ae, at most as wide as the flat part of the tip) and allowance – empty, the grey suggestions from the application apply.
  • climb milling only (lift, start again): off, the cutter runs the rows back and forth, every other one conventional. On, it runs every row from the front towards the chuck, lifts at its end and enters again at the front of the next; it takes the rows across in the order that puts the material on the climb side (the other way with M4).
  • The rows stop before walls and before the cylinder next to the face – calculated against the part without the face; past the end of the face the cutter only runs where nothing stands higher than the face itself (a step down, the end of the bar). Entry is a ramp at the cutter's plunge angle, or straight down where the row starts in front of the bar. After “All-round roughing” in the job the layers start where material is left.
  • Keyway: if the face is the floor of a slot – two parallel walls, half circles at the ends – it mills it like the “slot” in the block: with a cutter as wide as the slot at full width along a zigzag ramp (at a lower feed), with a narrower one in arcs and finally the wall once all round – right to the ends. The line says “– 1 of them as a slot: …”. A wide flat between two walls still gets rows.
  • Cross hole: if you click a hole across the bar (its cylindrical face), the rotary axis faces its opening and an end mill mills it like “hole milling” in the block – down in a helix, the wall all round; if it does not pass through the centre, Y moves across as well. A through hole is milled from both sides, each to the middle of the bar. The sentence above says “a hole across the bar”, the line “– N of them as a hole across the bar”.
  • Slot on the outside: if you click the floor of a slot running around the bar (a cylinder about the axis over part of the circumference, the ends walls through the axis – made with “Groove” or “Revolution”), the rotary axis turns and the cutter runs along the slot: as wide as the slot in full width down a zigzag ramp, narrower in rows to the walls as well. The sentence above says “a slot on the outside of the bar”, the line “– 1 of them as a slot on the outside of the bar”. At the ends a narrow wedge stays at the top: an end mill on the centre cannot cut walls through the axis exactly.
  • Radial drilling: if you have a drill with the diameter of the holes (tool type “Drill”, use “Drilling”) and click only cross holes, it is preselected under “Cutter” (“Suggestion: on – drill radially with …”): the rotary axis faces the opening, the drill goes down – pecking deeper than 3 × D – and the operation is called “Radial drilling T2”. A through hole is drilled from both sides, each with the point a little past the middle; a blind hole only if the point of a drill with its angle is drawn at the bottom (a flat bottom needs a cutter). Step-down and row spacing are then greyed out; the line says “→ 2 holes drilled (from 3 sides), 3 pecks, about … min”. You can choose a cutter again under “Cutter” – it then mills the holes in a helix.
  • Cutter and drill at once: if you click cross holes as well as faces or a slot and there is a drill of their diameter, the cutter stays under “Cutter” and below it “Drill the cross holes with … (own operation “Radial drilling”)” is ticked. “Create” then puts two operations into the same job: “Indexed facing T1” for faces and slot, “Radial drilling T2” for the holes. The line adds “Plus T2: 1 hole drilled …”. Untick it and the cutter mills the holes as well.
  • In grey below: “→ 2 layers, 6 rows, about 1 min”. The operation records faces, layers and rows; “Check on machine” runs it with Y and removes stock with the offset.

Step 2: all-round deburring

Breaks the outer edges of the selected faces – every edge where a selected face bends outwards into another face, also into the rest of the part: the long edge of a flat to the cylinder, the arc on top of its wall, the shoulder of a step. A chamfer mill runs along it, the rotary axis turns with it: every point of the edge comes under the cutter, which sinks on the ray from the axis until it touches the edge, and the chamfer width deeper. A ball mill rounds the edge instead. It runs every edge climb milling: the two faces at the edge tell where the material lies, the direction follows from that (the other way with M4).

  • It is suggested ticked as soon as there are outer edges and a chamfer mill with cutting data is in the tool manager – the reason is shown in grey below. With only a ball mill the tick stays off, and the sentence says it rounds the edges if you set it.
  • Cutter – chamfer or ball mill; Application – “Chamfering” preselected, it gives speed and feed.
  • Chamfer width – how wide the chamfer gets at a right-angled edge with a 90° chamfer mill (with a flat tip, wider by its radius on the flat side), or the edge break with the ball; empty 0.3 mm.
  • What has no burr stays: inner edges, fillets that blend tangentially. The end faces front and rear do not exist in the job yet (facing and parting come later) – their edges do not count. Where the cutter cannot reach an edge (in the shadow of a step, in a narrow slot, too close to the chuck) it skips it; the operation counts them under “Ausgelassen”.
  • From edge to edge in rapid; where one begins where the previous one ended, it continues without lifting – once around a flat with its walls. A ring all round (the shoulder of a step) is one turn of the rotary axis.
  • In grey below: “→ 4 edges, about 1 min”. “Check on machine” lets the operation have its chamfer – that deep at the edges is not blue –, and the collision check knows it is meant to cut into the finished part.

Changing it later

Double-click “All-round roughing …”, “All-round finishing …”, “Indexed facing …” or “All-round deburring …” in the tree – or right-click, Edit; or select the operation or its job and press the “4-axis machining” button – to open step 2 with the cutter, application and values of the operation. Empty fields follow the suggestion of the application: pick another cutter and its depth of cut applies.

  • Apply changes the operation – one undo step. Another cutter gets its own tool controller; the old one is removed when no operation uses it any more. The operation's name follows the tool: “All-round roughing T1” becomes “… T2” (a name of your own stays).
  • Speed and feed belong to the application in the tool manager: Tool manager …, change and save there, then Apply.
  • Back leads to step 1: bar, centre, rotation, facing allowance, part-off width, clamping length and rotary axis as they are in the job. What you change there shows in the 3D view at once; Next and Apply store it as an undo step of its own (“Change bar”) before the change of the operation. The part stays the same – another face of it can be clicked. If someone changed the job's stock or part by hand, step 1 stays closed; the window says so.
  • Adding finishing: when changing “All-round roughing”, “All-round finishing” is free as long as the job has none – tick it, Apply adds it. That is how a job from an earlier version gets its finishing.
  • Cancel changes nothing.

What happens in the document

  • A new job “Part – 4 axes”. The part lies in the bar as the job's model. The original stays where it was and is hidden.
  • The job's stock is a cylinder with the bar's diameter and length. While the window is open it is see-through and cannot be clicked. Afterwards it looks like any stock in CAM.
  • With “All-round roughing” also a tool controller “T1 Roughing” with spindle speed and feed from the tool manager and the operation “All-round roughing T1”, with “All-round finishing” likewise “T2 Finishing” and “All-round finishing T2”. The wizard removes the controller FreeCAD gives every new job.

What does not work (yet)

  • Milling a flat really flat (“indexed facing”) and deburring edges – that comes in a next stage. Until then the spiral mills it with the cutter pointing at the axis: where it is not straight above the flat, a little more stays.
  • Turning, facing the end and parting off are done by the machine – FreeCAD does not turn.
