Compare strategies

The same cutter can remove material in very different ways – wide and shallow, or narrow and deep. Which way is better is shown by the numbers. This window puts two use cases side by side, A (orange) and B (blue), and states below in sentences what follows from them.

The numbers

  • Removal per minute (Q) and time for 100 cm³ – how fast the material is gone.
  • Edge travel per cm³ – how far every point of the cutting edge travels through the material to remove 1 cm³. Less means less wear.
  • Flutes used – how much of the flute length is working. The wear is spread over that much of the edge.
  • Tooth in the material – which part of a revolution each tooth cuts. It cools down for the rest of the revolution.
  • Largest chip thickness – too thin and the edge rubs; too thick and it chips.
  • Cutting power – rough, from the material's kc1.1 value, without machine efficiency; plus the torque. Only for materials with a kc1.1 value.

All use cases at a glance

At the bottom of the window all use cases of the table are listed with the same figures. Bold is the best per column: most removal, least time and edge travel, most edge used. A and B are marked in their colour; a click on a row takes it as B.

Why edge travel

Per revolution the cutter removes ae · ap · fz · z. Every point of the edge that is engaged travels the arc D/2 · φ through the material (φ is the engagement angle). 1 cm³ needs 1000 / (ae · ap · fz · z) revolutions. Together:

Edge travel per cm³ = D · φ / (2 · ae · ap · fz · z) (in m, with mm inserted)

Wear grows roughly with this distance. It is a rule of thumb: it assumes the same vc – a higher vc wears the edge faster – and heat, chip evacuation, vibration and the rigidity of machine and setup all play a part.

Example: Ø 12, 3 flutes, vc 120 m/min

B removes 2.5 times as much per minute, and every point of the edge travels 12 times less through the material: the wear is spread over 25 instead of 3 mm of edge, and each tooth cools down for 90 % of the revolution instead of 50 %.

What follows

  • Roughing outer contours and pockets: narrow and deep rather than wide and shallow. In FreeCAD CAM this is Adaptive: the step over is ae, the step down is ap. It needs a rigid setup, a short stick-out, a machine that manages the feed rate and the many changes of direction – and the chips must get out.
  • Opening up holes and pockets: helically plunge a starting hole once, then widen it level by level with a large ap – as much of the edge as possible – and a small ae. That is faster and wears less than many shallow passes over the whole depth. How to do this in FreeCAD is described in the tool manager help under “Opening up a hole or pocket”.
  • Full slot only where it has to be a slot – then shallow, and mind the chips that can jam in the slot.
  • Finishing: small ae, ap over the full wall height – one cutting edge instead of many steps.
